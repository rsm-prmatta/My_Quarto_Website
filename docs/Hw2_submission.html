Does Raising Minimum Wage Kill Jobs?

Author

Prabhlin Kaur Matta

Published

April 19, 2026

A simple natural experiment from New Jersey challenges a classic economic assumption.

At first glance, the story seems obvious: if labor becomes more expensive, firms should hire fewer workers.

Card and Krueger’s landmark 1994 study shows why real-world outcomes often defy textbook predictions. In fast-food restaurants across New Jersey and eastern Pennsylvania, the data pointed in the opposite direction.

Why This Question Matters

Minimum wage debates typically begin with a clean theoretical prediction and end with a messier empirical reality. The standard competitive model holds that a higher wage floor raises the cost of labor, prompting firms to hire fewer workers. This intuition is straightforward, and for decades it shaped how economists and policymakers approached the issue.

Card and Krueger’s study challenges this consensus in a compelling way. When New Jersey raised its minimum wage from $4.25 to $5.05 in April 1992, neighboring eastern Pennsylvania did not follow suit. This policy divergence created a natural experiment: two geographically adjacent labor markets, one policy change, and a straightforward way to observe what happened next.

The key question is whether employment in New Jersey fell relative to Pennsylvania after the increase. If it did, the standard story looks stronger. If it did not, then the theory is at least incomplete in this setting.

How We Measure the Impact

The Difference-in-Differences (DID) estimator asks a deceptively simple question: how much did the treatment group change relative to the control group? By comparing changes rather than levels, this method controls for any pre-existing differences between states.

Here:

  • New Jersey is the treatment group.
  • Pennsylvania is the control group.
  • “Before” is the pre-policy survey.
  • “After” is the post-policy survey.

The formula is:

[ = (NJ_{after} - NJ_{before}) - (PA_{after} - PA_{before}) ]

To isolate the policy’s effect, the comparison must strip out any broad economic trends that would have affected both states regardless of the wage change. DID accomplishes this by using Pennsylvania as a benchmark for what New Jersey’s trajectory would have looked like absent the policy. It asks not just whether employment changed, but whether it changed more in New Jersey than in Pennsylvania.

What the Data Shows

Contrary to the standard prediction, the data do not tell a job-loss story. New Jersey employment edges up from 20.44 to 21.03 full-time-equivalent workers per store, while Pennsylvania falls from 23.33 to 21.17.

That means:

  • New Jersey changes by +0.59
  • Pennsylvania changes by -2.16
  • The DID estimate is 2.75

This result is confirmed by a simple 2×2 regression, which makes the DID logic explicit in equation form:

The interaction term is 2.75, exactly matching the DID calculation.

This result challenges the standard economic prediction that a higher minimum wage must reduce employment.

Seeing the Pattern

Before looking at the estimate itself, it helps to see the two state trends side by side. The first chart shows average employment before and after the policy in New Jersey and Pennsylvania.

Pennsylvania trends downward, but New Jersey does not follow the same path after the wage increase.

The second chart makes the comparison even sharper by focusing only on the change. This is where the DID logic becomes visual.

One state moves down, the other slightly up. The distance between those bars is the core result.

This is where things get especially interesting. The treatment state does not merely avoid a collapse. Relative to the control state, it looks stronger after the policy change.

A Closer Look Inside New Jersey

Card and Krueger go further by examining variation within New Jersey. By grouping restaurants according to their pre-policy wage levels, they can test whether the policy hit hardest where it should have: stores already paying the lowest wages. These establishments faced the largest mandated increase and, under the standard model, should have cut jobs most aggressively.

Instead, those stores show positive employment growth.

The lowest-wage New Jersey stores were the most exposed to the policy, yet they do not show the pattern of sharp job cuts that the simplest model would predict.

Why This Design Is Persuasive

The strength of the DID design is that it compares changes over time, not just raw snapshots, so the two states do not need to start from identical baselines.

What matters is whether Pennsylvania offers a believable benchmark for what New Jersey would have looked like without the policy change.

That logic works here because:

  • the states are geographically close
  • the restaurants are in the same industry
  • the data compare the same before-and-after window
  • the control group helps absorb broader economic changes

No observational design is perfect, and critics have raised questions about whether Pennsylvania truly serves as a valid counterfactual. But the design is credible precisely because it does not rely on New Jersey’s trend in isolation — it uses a geographically close, industry-matched control group to absorb economy-wide shocks.

What This Means

This case study illustrates a broader lesson: theoretical predictions, however intuitive, must be tested against data. The competitive model’s forecast was clear, but the evidence pointed in a different direction.

The headline estimate is 2.75, and the broader conclusion is hard to miss: employment in New Jersey did not fall relative to Pennsylvania after the minimum wage increase. If anything, the relative pattern goes the other way.

This finding does not imply that minimum wage increases are always employment-neutral or positive. Context matters — labor market structure, the size of the wage increase, and local economic conditions all play a role. What this study does establish is that the simplest textbook model is insufficient to explain what happened in New Jersey in 1992, and that the empirical question deserves more nuance than the theory alone provides.
